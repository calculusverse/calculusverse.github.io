Instantaneous Velocity: The Speedometer Secret

Why average speed isn't enough when you want to know how fast something is traveling at an exact, frozen millisecond in time.

The Real-Life Problem

A test car accelerates down a straight proving ground. Sensors record its position $s(t)$ (in meters) as a function of time $t$ (in seconds):

$$s(t) = 2t^2 + 4t$$

Target Questions:
1. What was the car's average speed over the interval from $t = 2\text{ s}$ to $t = 4\text{ s}$?
2. What was the car's exact speedometer reading (instantaneous velocity) at precisely $t = 3\text{ s}$?

🚗 Distance $s(t)$ vs ⏰ Time $t$
🚗
Time: t = 3.0s
Position: s = 30.0 m
Instant Speed: v = 16.0 m/s

$$v(3.0) = 4(3) + 4 = 16.0\text{ m/s}$$

Step-by-Step Mathematical Solution

Part 1: Average Velocity between $t=2$ and $t=4$

In traditional algebra, average velocity is simply the slope of the secant line between the two endpoints:

$$v_{\text{avg}} = \frac{s(4) - s(2)}{4 - 2}$$

Calculate positions:
• $s(4) = 2(4)^2 + 4(4) = 32 + 16 = 48\text{ m}$
• $s(2) = 2(2)^2 + 4(2) = 8 + 8 = 16\text{ m}$
Therefore:

$$v_{\text{avg}} = \frac{48 - 16}{2} = \frac{32}{2} = 16\text{ m/s}$$

Part 2: Instantaneous Velocity at $t = 3$ (The Derivative)

To know the speed at the single instant $t=3$, we take the limit as the time interval $\Delta t$ approaches zero:

$$v(t) = \frac{ds}{dt} = \lim_{\Delta t \to 0} \frac{s(t + \Delta t) - s(t)}{\Delta t}$$

Applying the Power Rule of differentiation:

$$\frac{d}{dt}[2t^2 + 4t] = 2(2t) + 4 = 4t + 4$$

Now, evaluate at $t = 3$:

$$v(3) = 4(3) + 4 = 12 + 4 = \mathbf{16\text{ m/s}}$$

✨ Notice how the average velocity over $[2, 4]$ exactly matches the instantaneous velocity at the midpoint $t=3$! This is an example of the Mean Value Theorem in action.
